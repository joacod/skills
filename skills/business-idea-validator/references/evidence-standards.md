## 1. Evidence states

Label every important conclusion:

- **Fact:** Directly supported by a reliable source or user-supplied observed result.
- **Inference:** A reasoned conclusion from facts. State the reasoning briefly.
- **Assumption:** A plausible input used provisionally because evidence is missing.
- **Unknown:** Consequential information that cannot yet be established.

Do not convert assumptions into facts through repetition. Use “insufficient evidence” when appropriate.

## 2. Demand evidence hierarchy

Prefer stronger behavior over weaker signals:

1. Payment, deposit, signed pilot, purchase order, or contract
2. Repeated paid usage, renewal, expansion, or referral to a budget owner
3. Existing budget, service fees, labor cost, losses, penalties, or procurement
4. Repeated costly workaround using staff, consultants, spreadsheets, or manual operations
5. Active search, detailed complaints, job postings, or requests for help
6. Stated interest in interviews or surveys
7. Free signups, clicks, likes, search volume, trend data, or compliments

Treat levels 6–7 as weak evidence unless corroborated. Desk research can establish plausibility and prioritize tests; it cannot prove willingness to pay.

## 3. Source hierarchy

Prioritize:

1. Primary laws, regulations, government guidance, public records, procurement, and court or agency material
2. Official product pages, pricing, documentation, APIs, integrations, security, terms, policies, and filings
3. Direct customer behavior: detailed reviews, complaints, support forums, professional discussions, job posts, operational templates, service pricing, and workflow artifacts
4. Credible trade associations, interviews, case studies, research papers, and operational benchmarks
5. Reputable secondary reporting for context

Use vendor pages to establish what a product claims, charges, supports, or targets—not to prove effectiveness, customer satisfaction, or market demand.

Do not rely on search-result snippets when the underlying source can be opened.

## 4. Freshness and current claims

Use current web research for information that may change, including competitors, pricing, product capabilities, APIs, policies, leadership, laws, standards, schedules, and market recommendations.

- Prefer current official pages for product and pricing facts.
- Use current primary sources for legal and regulatory claims.
- Use recent customer evidence when describing present workflows or dissatisfaction.
- Use historical evidence only when testing persistence, invariants, or prior market behavior.
- Include exact dates when changes, recency, or sequence matters.

When the user prohibits browsing or current evidence is unavailable, state that the result is preliminary and lower confidence.

## 5. Competition and market evidence

Map representative substitutes rather than claiming exhaustive coverage. Include direct products, broad suites, services, internal labor, generic tools, AI plus prompt when relevant, DIY, and doing nothing.

For each representative alternative, verify as available:

- Customer and use case
- Workflow and outcome
- Pricing or business model
- Implementation model
- Trust or responsibility assumed
- Current status

Interpret market density carefully:

- Many competitors can confirm demand but weaken differentiation.
- Few competitors can indicate whitespace or lack of demand.
- Customer complaints can reveal a wedge but do not establish purchase intent.
- Generic market-growth statistics do not prove a specific buyer will pay.

## 6. Contradictions and uncertainty

Preserve contradictory evidence. For example:

- Customers complain but continue renewing.
- A process is expensive but rare.
- A product has many competitors but a narrow underserved segment.
- Buyers report urgency but refuse meaningful commitment.

For each contradiction:

1. State both sides.
2. Identify whether they concern different segments, triggers, geographies, or time periods.
3. Explain the decision implication.
4. Propose the cheapest evidence that would resolve it.

Base confidence on source quality, directness, recency, corroboration, and consistency—not on the amount of prose produced.

## 7. Prohibited shortcuts

Do not:

- Use TAM, CAGR, search volume, social engagement, or AI adoption as proof of willingness to pay.
- Invent pricing, APIs, partnerships, legal permissions, conversion rates, or buyer behavior.
- Present synthetic interviews or model opinions as customer evidence.
- Claim a moat because data is proprietary without testing lawfulness, refresh, reproducibility, workflow integration, and economic effect.
- Claim regulation or operational complexity is attractive without measuring pre-scale burden and liability.
- Claim AI can automate a workflow without examining exceptions, error tolerance, evaluation, and accountability.
- Use a weighted score to hide a fatal flaw.
- Treat a one-time decision as subscription revenue without identifying a repeat or portfolio buyer.
