## 1. Validation principle

Design the smallest test that can produce stronger evidence than more research. Test the most decision-critical unknown, not the easiest activity.

A validation plan must specify:

- Exact prospect profile
- A 10–20 person qualified sample; assume the user handles recruitment
- Concrete offer and outcome
- Delivery method, including manual work
- Real price or meaningful commitment
- Evidence to collect
- Pass, iterate, and kill thresholds

Do not require production software when a manual, concierge, prototype, spreadsheet, or service can test the same assumption.

## 2. Match the test to the riskiest assumption

### Pain and urgency

Offer a paid diagnostic, audit, or manual resolution of the problem. Observe whether qualified prospects share real workflows, prioritize the problem, and pay to address it.

### Willingness to pay

Ask for payment, deposit, signed paid pilot, purchase order, or contract. Avoid asking only “would you pay?”

### Buyer and workflow clarity

Test the offer with the actual user, buyer, or budget owner and verify their roles, trigger, current workflow, and decision criteria. Do not treat reactions from people outside the workflow as validation.

### Wedge

Deliver the narrow outcome manually and ask customers to compare it with their current method or existing product. Capture why they would switch, add, or refuse.

### Effectiveness

Run the workflow on real data or a real case. Measure time, error, completion, revenue, cost, or risk before and after.

### Trust or permission

Ask for the actual access the product would need: data, documents, integration, authorization, review, or security approval. A prospect unwilling to grant necessary access reveals a material barrier.

### Recurrence and retention

Seek a second use, renewal, monitoring request, portfolio rollout, or another case from the same buyer. Do not infer subscription strength from one successful transaction.

## 3. Meaningful commitments

Use this rough hierarchy:

1. Paid renewal, repeated use, or expansion
2. Payment, deposit, signed paid pilot, purchase order, or contract
3. Access to real data, workflow, documents, staff time, or a budget owner
4. Referral to another qualified buyer or decision maker
5. Scheduled follow-up tied to a concrete case
6. Stated interest
7. Signup, click, like, or compliment

Choose a commitment proportional to the intended sale. A low-price self-serve product may use immediate payment; a higher-trust B2B product may require a signed pilot and real workflow access.

## 4. Default 7–14 day design

1. Define one prospect segment and one trigger.
2. Run the test with 10–20 qualified participants from the target workflow; participant recruitment is outside this framework.
3. Write a one-sentence offer describing the outcome, not the technology.
4. Set a real price or deposit that tests the proposed economics.
5. Deliver the result manually or with lightweight tools.
6. Record current method, cost, frequency, objections, buying authority, alternatives, and trust requirements.
7. Measure whether the result is materially better than the current method.
8. Ask for the next commitment: payment, repeat use, referral, access, or rollout.
9. Update the affected gates.

## 5. Thresholds

Set thresholds before the test. Adapt to price and decision cycle, but keep them behavior-based.

A reasonable default for a founder-led early test is:

- **Pass:** At least two qualified prospects make the target meaningful commitment, or one does so and requests repeat or broader use; delivery produces the promised outcome without unacceptable manual burden.
- **Iterate:** One qualified prospect commits, or several expose the same urgent problem but reject the offer for a repairable reason such as scope, buyer, trust, or price.
- **Kill or pivot:** No qualified participant makes a meaningful commitment after 15–20 relevant evaluations and five substantive buyer conversations, or buyers consistently report satisfaction with a cheap substitute, low urgency, no budget, or an unremovable trust or legal barrier.

Do not mechanically apply these numbers to enterprise procurement, consumer self-serve, or highly regulated sales. Explain any adaptation.

## 6. Common invalid tests

Do not use these alone as validation:

- General surveys
- Friends' opinions
- Social engagement
- Free signups
- Landing-page traffic
- Waitlists without payment
- Synthetic interviews
- Feature preference questions
- A prototype shown to non-buyers
- Positive feedback without a next commitment

A landing page or prototype can support a test, but the decisive evidence must be behavioral.

## 7. Updating the verdict

After the test:

1. Classify results as facts, inferences, assumptions, or unknowns.
2. Update only the gates the test addressed.
3. Lower confidence when the sample is unqualified or results conflict.
4. Raise confidence when behavior repeats across qualified buyers.
5. Do not add features to explain away a failed demand test.
6. Pivot only when evidence identifies a specific repair.
7. Drop the idea when repeated meaningful tests fail the same gate.
