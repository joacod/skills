## 1. Seven decision gates

### 1. Pain and urgency

Ask whether a specific trigger creates a costly, risky, delayed, or revenue-relevant consequence.

### 2. Existing spend or willingness to pay

Look for budgets, fees, labor, losses, penalties, paid substitutes, or meaningful commitments.

### 3. Buyer and workflow clarity

Ask whether the buyer, user, budget owner, trigger, current workflow, and adoption decision are specific enough to evaluate. A compelling idea should identify who receives value, who authorizes payment, and where the product fits without relying on vague labels such as “small businesses” or “everyone.”

### 4. Wedge and differentiation

Ask whether a narrow reason exists to choose this offer over focused competitors, broad platforms, services, generic tools, AI plus prompt, DIY, or doing nothing.

### 5. Economics and recurrence

Ask whether pricing can support delivery, onboarding, support, and continued use. Distinguish recurring, usage, transaction, setup, implementation, and service revenue.

### 6. Feasibility and owner burden

Ask whether a small team can deliver reliably without unacceptable regulation, liability, customization, capital, security, uptime, support, or on-call work.

### 7. Durability

Ask whether value survives cheaper and better technology, replication, substitution, platform changes, and plausible incumbent response.

## 2. Signal anchors

Use these anchors consistently:

| Signal | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `STRONG` | Direct, current evidence supports the gate; risks are bounded and no major unresolved contradiction remains |
| `MIXED` | Some credible support exists, but material limitations, segment dependence, or contradictory evidence remains |
| `WEAK` | Evidence points against the gate, or a major obstacle is already visible |
| `UNKNOWN` | Evidence is insufficient; do not infer a positive or negative result |

Do not assign `STRONG` from a plausible story alone.

## 3. Fatal flaws

A fatal flaw can determine the verdict regardless of other strengths. Examples include:

- No identifiable buyer or trigger
- Problem lacks meaningful consequence
- Buyer, user, or workflow remains too vague to form a testable offer
- Cheap, focused alternatives already solve the problem with no credible wedge
- Required legal permission, data, API, partnership, capital, or trust cannot plausibly be obtained
- Liability, security, support, or implementation cost overwhelms revenue
- Product requires network effects before delivering standalone value
- Value disappears when enabling technology becomes cheap or bundled
- Validation itself requires substantial infrastructure or capital

Do not average a fatal flaw into a medium score.

## 4. Verdict rules

### `PURSUE`

Use when the critical gates are `STRONG` or well-bounded `MIXED`, no fatal flaw exists, and evidence supports moving directly to a paid pilot or implementation. This does not mean “build a full product.”

### `TEST`

Use when the idea is credible but one or two high-impact assumptions remain unresolved and can be tested cheaply within 7–14 days.

### `PIVOT`

Use when the underlying buyer, problem, workflow, or market contains evidence, but the current buyer, scope, delivery model, economics, or wedge is materially weak. Name the repaired version.

### `PARK`

Use when the idea may become attractive but current timing, founder constraints, evidence, regulation, or test cost makes it irrational to pursue now. State the event or evidence that should trigger reconsideration.

### `DROP`

Use when a fatal flaw is established, the idea is below the user's opportunity cost, or repeated meaningful tests fail. State the decisive reason without softening it.

## 5. Confidence

Assign confidence based on evidence quality and convergence:

- **High:** Strong direct evidence, current sources, minimal contradiction, and a clear decision implication.
- **Medium:** Credible but incomplete evidence; one or two assumptions could still alter the result.
- **Low:** Mostly inference, weak proxies, inaccessible evidence, or unresolved contradictions.

Confidence describes the verdict's evidentiary support, not enthusiasm for the idea.

## 6. Crowding classifications

Use one of these descriptions:

- **Validated but undifferentiated:** Spending is visible, but the idea lacks a compelling wedge.
- **Crowded with a credible wedge:** Many alternatives exist, yet a specific buyer, trigger, workflow, outcome, or responsibility remains underserved.
- **Fragmented or underserved:** Alternatives are broad, manual, service-heavy, local, or poorly coordinated, and a focused outcome may be sellable.
- **Sparse and unproven:** Few alternatives exist and demand evidence is weak; absence of competition is not positive evidence.

Crowding is not a verdict by itself. Combine it with spend, buyer and workflow clarity, and wedge.

## 7. Pivot quality

A strong pivot:

- Repairs a named `WEAK` or `UNKNOWN` gate.
- Changes no more than two variables.
- Preserves the useful underlying insight.
- Produces a more testable buyer, trigger, outcome, or delivery model.
- Has a distinct failure condition.

A weak pivot:

- Adds features or AI.
- Changes branding only.
- Moves to a random industry.
- Assumes customer behavior will change without a concrete reason.
- Avoids the real fatal flaw.
